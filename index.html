What is phishing?

Phishing is a cyber attack where someone pretends to be a trusted person or organization (a bank, your boss, a delivery company) to trick you into giving up passwords, money, or personal data, or into installing malware. It usually arrives by email, but the same trick is used over other channels.

Email phishing

Mass emails that imitate banks, shops, or online services.

Spear phishing

Targeted emails that use your name, job, or colleagues to look genuine.

Smishing

The same attack through SMS or messaging apps, often with a short link.

Vishing

Phone calls from a fake “bank” or “IT support” asking for codes or access.

Whaling and BEC

Fake messages from executives asking staff for urgent payments or data.

Clone and fake sites

Look-alike login pages built to capture what you type.

Check the sender

Look at the real address, not the display name. Watch for swapped letters (paypa1) and odd domains.

Check the tone

Urgency, threats, and prizes are designed to make you act before you think.

Check links and files

Hover over links before clicking. Be careful with ZIP, EXE, and Office files with macros.

How to recognize a fake website

Attackers copy the look of a real site perfectly. The only thing they cannot copy is the real domain name. Read the address from right to left, up to the first single slash.

The padlock is not proof

HTTPS only means the connection is encrypted. Phishing sites can have it too.

Other warning signs

Spelling mistakes, low-quality logos, pop-ups asking for card details, and pages reached only through an emailed link.

Safer habit

Type the address yourself or use a saved bookmark, then sign in from there.

Social engineering tactics

Phishing works on people, not machines. Attackers rely on a few psychological levers.

Urgency

“Act now or lose access.” Time pressure stops careful thinking.

Fear

Fake security alerts, legal threats, or unpaid fines.

Authority

Messages that appear to come from the CEO, police, or IT department.

Greed and curiosity

Prizes, refunds, “see who viewed your profile”.

Trust and helpfulness

A “colleague” asking for a quick favor, or a stranger who seems friendly.

Pretexting and baiting

An invented story to justify a request, or a USB drive left lying around.

Real-world examples

Google and Facebook invoice scam (2013 to 2015)A man impersonated a real hardware supplier and sent fake invoices to employees of both companies. They paid, and the total losses were reported at over 100 million dollars.
Twitter hack (July 2020)Attackers phoned Twitter employees, posing as internal IT, and obtained access to internal tools. High-profile accounts were then taken over to promote a cryptocurrency scam.
RSA breach (2011)A spear-phishing email with an infected Excel attachment reached a few employees. One opened it, which gave attackers a way into RSA’s network.
Target breach (2013)Attackers first compromised a third-party heating and cooling vendor, then used its stolen credentials to reach Target’s network, leading to the theft of payment card data.

Lesson: even large, security-aware companies were fooled. One careless click or one trusted phone call is enough.

Best practices to stay safe

  • Pause and verify. Contact the sender through a known phone number or official site, never through details in the message.
  • Hover before you click. Check where a link really goes. When unsure, don’t click.
  • Never share passwords, one-time codes, or card details by email, chat, or phone.
  • Turn on multi-factor authentication on every important account.
  • Use a password manager and a unique password per site. It will not auto-fill on a fake domain.
  • Don’t open unexpected attachments, especially ZIP, EXE, and Office files asking to “enable macros”.
  • Keep software and antivirus updated, and use your email provider’s spam filter.
  • Report it. Forward suspicious emails to your IT or security team, use the “Report phishing” button, then delete.

If you already clicked

1. Disconnect from the network if you opened a file.

2. Change the affected passwords from a safe device.

3. Tell your IT team or bank immediately.

4. Watch accounts for strange activity.
